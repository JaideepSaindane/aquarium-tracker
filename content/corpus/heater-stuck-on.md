symptoms:
  - Water noticeably warmer than the heater's set temperature, often confirmed by a separate thermometer disagreeing with the heater's display/dial
  - Fish gasping at the surface, lethargic, or showing red/inflamed gills
  - The heater's indicator light staying on continuously rather than cycling off once the set temperature is reached
likely_causes:
  - A failed thermostat inside the heater, stuck in the "on" position regardless of actual water temperature
  - A cheap or aging heater with no reliable cutoff, common with older or low-cost units
  - A heater not fully submerged or positioned near a still-water zone, causing it to misread the surrounding temperature and keep heating
immediate_actions:
  - Unplug the heater immediately — this is the single most urgent step and takes priority over everything else.
  - Test the water with a separate, independent thermometer to confirm the actual temperature, not the heater's own display.
  - If the water is significantly overheated, perform a partial water change with cooler dechlorinated water to bring the temperature down gradually rather than in one large drop.
  - Increase surface agitation/aeration immediately — warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, compounding the crisis for fish already stressed by heat.
  - Once temperature is stabilised, replace the heater entirely — do not reuse a heater that has stuck on, even if it "seems fine" after unplugging and cooling. Never plug it back in for a species check.
do_not_do:
  - Do not cool an overheated tank with a large, fast temperature drop — sudden swings are their own stressor; bring temperature down gradually via partial water changes.
  - Do not reuse a heater that stuck on, even if it appears to work again once unplugged and retested outside the tank — a thermostat that has already failed once is unreliable.
  - Do not rely solely on a heater's own built-in display/dial to judge actual tank temperature — always confirm with an independent thermometer.

A heater stuck on is a fast-moving emergency because warm water holds progressively less dissolved oxygen
even as the fish's metabolic demand for oxygen rises with the heat — the two effects compound each other
quickly. The first and most urgent action is always unplugging the heater, before doing anything else,
including checking on the fish.

Independent verification matters here specifically because a heater with a failed thermostat cannot be
trusted to report its own temperature accurately — a separate thermometer is the only reliable way to know
how bad the situation actually is and to confirm the water has genuinely cooled back to a safe range before
considering the emergency over.

Cooling should still be gradual. An overheated tank is already a stressed system, and swinging temperature
down too fast with a very large, very cold water change adds a second shock on top of the first. Partial
water changes with cooler (not cold) dechlorinated water bring temperature down safely over a slightly
longer window. Once stable, the heater itself should be retired — a thermostat that has failed once has
demonstrated it cannot be trusted, regardless of how it tests afterward.
