Velvet, caused by the dinoflagellate parasite *Piscinoodinium*, is often confused with ich because both
present as a fine coating on the skin, but velvet progresses considerably faster and attacks the gills more
aggressively — a fish can go from first visible signs to critical respiratory distress within a day or two.
The coating itself is finer and dustier than ich's distinct raised white grains, often with a gold, rust or
yellow-brown tint that is easiest to see with a torch held at a low angle in a dim room.

An unusual and genuinely useful feature of this parasite's biology is that its free-living stage relies
partly on photosynthesis, so reducing light exposure — darkening the tank or the room — measurably slows
its lifecycle. This costs nothing and carries no risk, and is worth doing immediately alongside medication
rather than as an afterthought.

Because of how fast velvet moves, the "observe for a day and see if it's getting worse" approach that is
reasonable caution for milder issues is actively risky here. Gill damage in particular can progress to a
point where even successful parasite elimination does not save a badly affected fish, which is why prompt
treatment and, in bad cases, experienced guidance on the specific tank matter more than for slower diseases.
