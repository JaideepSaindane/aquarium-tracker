symptoms:
  - Fish that appeared fine (or only mildly unwell) for a long time in a tank that has not had regular maintenance
  - Fish dying or showing acute distress shortly after a first "proper" water change or cleanup in a long-neglected tank
  - Very low pH, high nitrate, and high dissolved mineral content typically found on testing an old-tank-syndrome tank
  - Fish that seem oddly tolerant of parameters that would normally be considered dangerous
likely_causes:
  - A tank that has gone a long time (often many months to years) with little or no water changes
  - Fish gradually adapting to slowly worsening water quality — acidic pH, high nitrate, high dissolved solids — because the change happened slowly enough for physiological adaptation
  - A well-meaning "let's finally clean this properly" large water change or full substrate clean that suddenly restores parameters to a much more different reading than the fish are adapted to
immediate_actions:
  - Test the current parameters fully (pH, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate, KH, GH) before doing anything else — you need a baseline of what the fish are actually adapted to, not what parameters "should" be.
  - If a large change or cleanup has already happened and fish are showing distress, treat it the same as any acute parameter swing: increase aeration, watch closely, and do NOT do another large change to try to "fix" it further — that compounds the shock.
  - If you have not yet done a big change: do not do one. Move to small water changes (10-15%) spaced days apart instead, re-testing between each one, and plan to bring the tank back to normal parameters over weeks, not in one session.
  - Rinse or clean filter media gently in removed tank water, never replace it all at once, to avoid also crashing the biofilter on top of the parameter shock.
  - If substrate has never been cleaned, clean it gradually over multiple sessions rather than disturbing it all at once, which can release a large amount of trapped waste into the water column at once.
do_not_do:
  - Do not do a single large water change (50%+) on a long-neglected tank as your first intervention. Fish physiologically adapted to poor water can go into fatal osmotic and pH shock when parameters move back toward "normal" too fast.
  - Do not do a full substrate vacuum/clean in one session on a tank that has never had one — this can release a large pulse of trapped ammonia and organic waste at once.
  - Do not replace all filter media at once alongside a big water change — losing the biofilter and getting a parameter swing at the same time is the worst-case combination.
  - Do not assume fish "looking fine" in a neglected tank means the tank is actually fine — chronic adaptation masks a real underlying problem that a sudden change will expose.
when_to_escalate:
  - Multiple fish showing distress after a change has already been made — this needs careful, slow stabilisation rather than further intervention; consider an experienced keeper's guidance on pacing the recovery.
  - Genuinely unknown tank history (e.g. an inherited or long-unattended tank) — treat it as old tank syndrome by default and go slow, even if the fish currently look fine.

Old tank syndrome is what happens when a tank goes without proper maintenance for a long time — often
months — and the fish inside it slowly, physiologically adapt to steadily worsening water: falling pH,
climbing nitrate, rising dissolved solids. Because the change happens gradually, the fish's body has time
to compensate, and they can look outwardly normal in water that would be immediately dangerous if it
appeared suddenly.

The danger is not the neglect itself — it is the well-intentioned fix. A keeper who discovers the tank has
been neglected, feels guilty, and does a single large "let's sort this out properly" water change or deep
clean is moving the fish's environment back toward "normal" faster than their adapted physiology can
handle. The resulting osmotic and pH shock is often what actually kills fish that had been quietly
surviving the neglect itself for a long time.

The correct response inverts the natural instinct: go slow. Bring the tank back to healthy parameters over
days to weeks with small, staged water changes and gradual substrate cleaning, testing between each step,
rather than treating "getting it back to normal" as a single urgent cleanup session.
