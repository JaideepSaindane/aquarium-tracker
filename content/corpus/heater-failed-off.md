symptoms:
  - Water temperature noticeably below the set point, confirmed with an independent thermometer
  - The heater's indicator light not coming on at all, or the heater cold to the touch
  - Fish sluggish, hiding, reduced appetite, or clustering in one area
likely_causes:
  - A failed heating element or thermostat that has stopped producing heat entirely
  - A heater unplugged, tripped on a GFCI/RCD outlet, or affected by a power cut (see corpus:power-cuts)
  - A heater undersized for the tank's actual volume or the room's ambient temperature, struggling especially during a cold spell
immediate_actions:
  - Confirm actual water temperature with an independent thermometer, not the heater's own dial.
  - Check the power source first (plug, switch, breaker/RCD) before assuming the heater itself has failed — this is the more common and cheaper fix.
  - If the heater has genuinely failed, warm the room if practical and consider a temporary backup heat source while a replacement is sourced, appropriate to the species' actual temperature tolerance.
  - Raise temperature back to the target gradually rather than all at once once a working heater is restored, to avoid a second temperature shock.
  - For a tropical species tank, treat any prolonged cold period as urgent — many popular tropical fish have a fairly narrow safe range and decline within a day or two of sustained low temperature.
do_not_do:
  - Do not assume a heater is the problem before checking the simpler causes first (unplugged, tripped breaker, power cut).
  - Do not warm the tank back up all at once with hot water — bring temperature up gradually, the same principle as cooling down from overheating.
  - Do not leave a cold-water event unaddressed overnight for a tropical species assuming "it'll be fine until morning" — several hours at a significantly low temperature can already be harmful.

A heater that has failed off is a slower-moving problem than one stuck on, but still genuinely urgent for a
tropical tank, since most popular tropical species have a fairly narrow comfortable temperature range and
decline measurably within a day or so of sustained cold. The first check should always be the power source
itself — an unplugged heater, a tripped breaker, or a power cut (corpus:power-cuts) are all more common and
cheaper explanations than an actually failed unit, and ruling them out first avoids buying an unnecessary
replacement.

If the heater has genuinely failed, temperature should still be restored gradually once a working heater is
back in place — the same reasoning that applies to cooling an overheated tank slowly applies here in
reverse. A sudden jump back to target temperature is its own stressor on top of the cold exposure that has
already occurred.

Keeping a spare heater on hand, or at minimum knowing where to source one quickly, is a reasonable
precaution for anyone keeping genuinely temperature-sensitive species, given how common routine power
interruptions and equipment failures both are.
