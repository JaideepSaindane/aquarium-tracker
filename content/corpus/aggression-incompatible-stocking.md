  - Torn or ragged fins with no disease pattern (contrast with fin rot, corpus:fin-rot, which is a different mechanism — check both if uncertain)

  - A fish hiding constantly, showing stress colouration, or losing condition despite the tank's water quality being fine

  - An incompatible species combination — genuinely aggressive or territorial species kept with peaceful/timid ones
  - A schooling species kept below its minimum group size, causing the group's natural social stress/aggression to redirect onto other tankmates or onto each other
  - Insufficient territory/space or hiding spots for the specific social structure of the species involved (e.g. cichlids needing defined territories)

  - Identify the specific aggressor and target, and observe the pattern (constant vs occasional, one-directional vs mutual) to distinguish genuine bullying from normal social hierarchy behaviour.
  - Check whether a schooling species is below its recommended minimum group size — this is a very common, fixable cause, and increasing the group (rather than removing fish) often resolves it.
  - Rearrange decor/add hiding spots and sightline breaks to reduce a territorial fish's ability to constantly access and harass others.
  - If a specific individual is the clear, persistent aggressor and rearranging does not help, consider rehoming that individual or the targeted victim to a more suitable setup.

  - Do not assume torn fins are automatically disease (fin rot) without first observing for behavioural aggression from tankmates — treating a bullying-caused injury purely as a water-quality/disease issue misses the actual cause and the injury will continue.

Aggression and incompatible stocking is a commonly underdiagnosed cause of injury and death that gets
attributed to disease instead, particularly when the symptom is torn or ragged fins — a presentation that
looks similar to fin rot (corpus:fin-rot) but has an entirely different cause and needs an entirely
different fix. Water quality correction and medication do nothing for an injury that a tankmate is actively
causing.

One specific, very common and very fixable cause deserves particular attention: a schooling species kept
below its recommended minimum group size. Many schooling fish rely on the group itself for a sense of
security, and when kept in too small a number, the resulting social stress often redirects as aggression
toward other tankmates or even within the under-sized group itself — increasing the school to its proper
size, rather than removing the stressed fish, is frequently the actual fix.

More broadly, diagnosing aggression means watching the tank directly — who is chasing whom, how
persistently, and whether it is one-directional bullying or normal, tolerable social hierarchy behaviour —
rather than assuming any injury must be disease-related. Correcting stocking (group size, species
compatibility, adequate territory and hiding spots) addresses the root cause in a way no medication can.
