Copper is one of the more effective treatments against several external parasites, including velvet, but
it is also one of the least forgiving medications in this corpus toward invertebrates — shrimp, snails and
other freshwater invertebrates have essentially no meaningful tolerance for copper at the concentration
needed to treat fish, making "just be a bit careful with the dose" not a viable safety strategy the way it
might be for a more forgiving medication.

Two practical consequences follow. First, copper treatment should happen in a hospital tank separate from
any invertebrates, or after fully removing them — there is no safe compromise dose that treats fish while
sparing shrimp or snails in the same water. Second, copper binds to substrate, decor, and filter media, and
can continue leaching out for a long period — weeks to months — after the active treatment ends, meaning a
display tank once treated with copper may not be genuinely safe to restock with invertebrates again without
specific remediation and testing to confirm it has actually cleared.

Required copper concentration also varies with water hardness, which is why dosing by a fixed recipe alone
is unreliable — a copper test kit, used to confirm and maintain the actual therapeutic level, is a
necessary part of using this medication correctly rather than an optional extra.
