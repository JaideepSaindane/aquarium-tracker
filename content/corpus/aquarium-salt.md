immediate_actions:
  - Before dosing salt for any reason, identify every species in the tank and check individual tolerance — "salt is natural/safe" is a dangerous oversimplification given how many common freshwater species tolerate it poorly.
  - Never add salt to a tank containing live plants without checking plant-specific tolerance — many common aquarium plants are salt-sensitive.
  - Use plain, non-iodised salt (aquarium salt, kosher salt, or plain pickling/canning salt) — never iodised table salt, which contains additives that can harm fish.
  - Dose gradually and dissolve fully before adding, rather than pouring undissolved salt directly into the tank.
do_not_do:
  - Do not add salt "just in case" as a general tonic — despite being widely recommended this way in the hobby, it is genuinely dangerous to a long list of common species and should be used for a specific, justified reason (e.g. nitrite spike treatment, a specific disease protocol) rather than routinely.
  - Do not add salt to a tank with most catfish, loaches, or other scaleless species without confirming that specific species' tolerance — many tolerate it poorly.
  - Do not add salt to a tank with shrimp, snails or other freshwater invertebrates — even modest doses can be harmful or fatal to sensitive species.
  - Do not add salt to a tank with electroreceptive fish (elephant nose fish, black ghost knife fish and similar) — these are notably salt-intolerant.
  - Do not assume a salt dose that is safe for one species in a mixed community tank is safe for all of them — check the most sensitive resident, not the hardiest.

Plain sodium chloride (non-iodised aquarium, kosher, or pickling salt — never iodised table salt) is a
genuinely useful tool in specific situations, most notably as an emergency treatment for nitrite poisoning
(see corpus:nitrite-spike, where chloride ions competitively block nitrite uptake at the gill). It is also
where the single most common and most dangerous fatal belief in freshwater fishkeeping lives: "salt is
natural, so it's always safe."

That belief does not hold for a large share of common freshwater community fish. Many catfish and loaches
(scaleless species generally), most soft-water tetras, freshwater shrimp, most snails, live plants, and
electroreceptive species like elephant nose and black ghost knife fish tolerate salt poorly to very poorly,
and a dose that is routine for a hardy livebearer community can be harmful or fatal to any of these in the
same tank.

The correct default is not "salt is generally good for fish" — it is "salt has specific, legitimate uses,
and every use needs the tank's actual stocking checked against it first." A mixed community tank should be
evaluated by its most sensitive resident before any salt is added, not its hardiest.
