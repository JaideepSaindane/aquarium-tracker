Mycobacteriosis — often called fish TB — is the one entry in this entire corpus that carries a genuine
human-health dimension, and that fact needs to be surfaced plainly rather than treated as a footnote. The
causative *Mycobacterium* species can, in rare cases, infect humans through contact between broken skin and
contaminated aquarium water, most often producing a localised skin infection sometimes called "fish
handler's disease" or "fish tank granuloma." The risk to a healthy person handling tank water normally with
gloves is low, but it is real, well documented in medical literature, and worth a plain, direct warning
rather than a vague mention.

For the fish itself, this is a chronic, slow-progressing, and realistically untreatable condition. It
presents as gradual wasting — weight loss despite normal appetite — sometimes with spinal curvature or skin
lesions in advanced cases, developing over weeks to months rather than presenting as an acute illness.
Definitive diagnosis needs lab testing that is not available to most home keepers, so this is usually a
presumptive diagnosis reached by ruling out other, more treatable causes of chronic wasting first.

Honesty matters most here: there is no dependable, established cure. Antibiotic treatment attempts exist in
the literature, but success rates are inconsistent enough that promising a cure to a worried keeper would
be misleading. The responsible guidance is humane management — isolating the affected fish, protecting
human health during any handling, and considering the fish's welfare, including euthanasia in advanced
cases, over an indefinite and unlikely-to-succeed treatment attempt.
