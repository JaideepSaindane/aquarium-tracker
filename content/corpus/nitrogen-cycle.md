symptoms:
  - New tank (under 6–8 weeks old) with ammonia and/or nitrite reading above 0
  - Cloudy water in a newly set-up tank (a bacterial bloom, often mistaken for dirty water)
  - Fish stress or deaths in a tank that has never had a full cycle
likely_causes:
  - The tank has not yet built up enough of the two bacteria groups needed to process fish waste
  - Fish were added before the biofilter had time to establish
  - An established biofilter was damaged or reset (see corpus:cycle-crash, corpus:stalled-cycle)
immediate_actions:
  - Understand this is a process to wait out and support, not a problem to medicate. If ammonia or nitrite is currently elevated, follow corpus:ammonia-spike or corpus:nitrite-spike for the immediate fix — this entry explains why that's happening and how long it takes to stop happening on its own.
  - Test ammonia, nitrite and nitrate regularly (every 1–2 days in a new tank) rather than guessing at cycle progress.
  - Feed the bacteria a steady, modest ammonia source — either from a light fish load already in the tank, or a fishless dosing source — rather than large irregular doses.
  - Do partial water changes to keep ammonia and nitrite from reaching harmful levels while the bacteria are still establishing — this does not "reset" the cycle; it protects the fish while it finishes.
  - Once ammonia and nitrite both read 0 and nitrate is present, the tank is cycled. Confirm this holds for at least a few days before treating the tank as fully established.
do_not_do:
  - Do not rinse filter media under the tap, or replace it outright, while cycling or afterwards — the bacteria live on the media's surface, and chlorinated tap water kills them.
  - Do not add a full fish load to an uncycled tank "to get it going faster" — this is fish-in cycling done carelessly and risks the exact ammonia/nitrite spikes this entry is describing (see corpus:fish-in-cycling for the safer version of this approach).
  - Do not trust a bottled "instant cycle" product to fully replace the 4–8 week establishment window without verifying with your own test kit (see corpus:bottled-bacteria-claims).
  - Do not panic and do a 100% water change the moment ammonia or nitrite appears — partial changes protect the fish without starving the bacteria of the ammonia they need to multiply.
when_to_escalate:
  - Ammonia or nitrite stays elevated for several weeks with no downward trend — see corpus:stalled-cycle for specific causes and fixes.
  - A previously cycled tank suddenly shows ammonia or nitrite again — see corpus:cycle-crash rather than assuming a fresh cycle is starting.

Fish waste, uneaten food and decaying plant matter break down into ammonia (NH3/NH4+), which is toxic even
at low concentrations. Two groups of naturally occurring bacteria colonise a tank's filter media, substrate
and any hard surface, and together they neutralise it in two steps. The first group oxidises ammonia into
nitrite (NO2-), which is also toxic — it interferes with a fish's ability to carry oxygen in its blood. The
second group oxidises that nitrite into nitrate (NO3-), which is comparatively low-toxicity at aquarium
concentrations and is removed mainly by water changes (see corpus:nitrate-creep for what happens when it
isn't).

This is "the cycle": ammonia → nitrite → nitrate. A brand-new tank has none of these bacteria in useful
numbers, and building up a colony large enough to keep pace with a tank's fish load takes roughly 4–8 weeks,
depending on temperature, the ammonia source's consistency, and how much surface area the filter provides
for bacteria to colonise. This is a biological process with its own timeline — no product or technique
skips it outright, though a few (see corpus:bottled-bacteria-claims, and seeding with media or gravel from
an established tank) can shorten it meaningfully.

Two ways to run this process exist. Fishless cycling (corpus:fishless-cycling) doses an ammonia source into
an empty tank and waits for the full ammonia→nitrite→nitrate progression before any fish go in — no livestock
is ever at risk. Fish-in cycling (corpus:fish-in-cycling) starts with a small, hardy fish load already
present and manages ammonia/nitrite with frequent water changes while the bacteria establish around them —
riskier, but it's where most beginners who didn't know about cycling in advance actually are, and it has its
own damage-limitation protocol rather than a lecture about what should have happened instead.

Once established, the biofilter is a living population that needs a continuous, roughly steady ammonia
source to stay at the size the tank needs — this is why it can crash from a long unfed period, a big
medication course, or chlorinated water hitting the media (corpus:cycle-crash), and why it's worth
understanding as an ongoing thing to protect, not a one-time task to finish and forget.
