immediate_actions:
  - Set up a separate, simple quarantine tank (bare-bottom or minimal decor for easy observation and cleaning, sized appropriately for the fish being held) before bringing new fish home whenever practical, rather than after a problem appears.
  - Cycle the quarantine tank's filtration in advance if possible (a spare, already-seeded sponge filter is a common practical solution) so newly added fish are not also facing an uncycled tank on top of everything else.
  - Hold new fish in quarantine for a meaningful observation period (commonly several weeks) before adding them to an established display tank, watching closely for any signs of disease during that window.
  - Use separate equipment (net, siphon) for the quarantine tank versus the display tank, or disinfect shared equipment between uses, to avoid cross-contaminating the display tank even while the new fish are still isolated.
  - Treat quarantine as the appropriate place to observe and, if needed, treat a new fish for anything that surfaces — see corpus:prophylactic-quarantine-treatment for the more involved preventive approach some keepers use during this window.
do_not_do:
  - Do not skip quarantine because a fish "looks healthy" at the point of purchase — many diseases and parasites have an incubation period and are not visible yet at that point.
  - Do not use shared, undisinfected equipment between the quarantine tank and the display tank — this defeats much of the purpose of keeping them separate in the first place.
  - Do not treat quarantine as optional only for "delicate" species — any newly acquired fish, however hardy the species is generally considered, can be carrying something not yet visible.

Quarantine exists to close the gap between "looks healthy at purchase" and "is actually free of anything
transmissible" — many of the diseases covered elsewhere in this corpus (ich, flukes, internal parasites,
various bacterial infections) have an incubation period during which an infected fish shows no visible
signs at all. A separate holding tank, kept simple and easy to observe and clean, gives that incubation
window time to pass before a new fish reaches an established display tank and its existing, otherwise
healthy stock.

Practical setup matters as much as the concept: a quarantine tank that is itself uncycled introduces a
second stressor (ammonia/nitrite exposure) on top of whatever the new fish is already adjusting to after
transport, which is why keeping a spare, pre-seeded filter (such as a sponge filter cycled alongside the
main display tank) specifically for quarantine use is a common and effective habit among experienced
keepers.

Cross-contamination through shared equipment is an easy way to accidentally defeat the entire purpose of
quarantine — a net or siphon used in the quarantine tank and then the display tank without disinfecting
between uses can transfer exactly the pathogens quarantine was meant to catch. Dedicated equipment, or a
simple disinfection step between uses, closes that gap.
