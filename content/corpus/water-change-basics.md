immediate_actions:
  - Perform routine partial water changes (commonly 20-30% weekly for most community tanks, adjusted for stocking level and nitrate readings) rather than large infrequent ones, for stability.
  - Always treat tap water with a dechlorinator/water conditioner rated for chlorine and chloramine (not chlorine alone — many municipal supplies use chloramine, which needs a conditioner specifically rated for it) before it touches the tank.
  - Match temperature reasonably closely between new water and tank water before adding it, to avoid an avoidable temperature shock.
  - Check the pH-ammonia interaction (corpus:ph-ammonia-trap) before any water change in a tank with both low pH and elevated ammonia — in that specific combination, standard water-change advice can become dangerous rather than helpful.
  - Vacuum substrate debris during water changes as routine maintenance, since accumulated waste is a slow-building source of the ammonia/nitrate load between changes.
do_not_do:
  - Do not add untreated tap water directly to a tank — chlorine and chloramine are both toxic to fish and to the biofilter.
  - Do not perform one very large water change as a substitute for regular smaller ones — big sudden parameter or temperature swings are themselves a stressor, and a large change on a neglected tank especially risks shocking fish that have slowly adapted to degraded conditions.
  - Do not skip checking the pH-ammonia interaction before a water change when both pH is low and ammonia is present — see corpus:ph-ammonia-trap for why this specific combination is an exception to normal water-change advice.

Regular partial water changes are the single most effective routine maintenance habit in the hobby — they
dilute accumulating nitrate and other dissolved waste products that biological filtration does not remove,
and they are far gentler on a tank's stability than infrequent, large changes. A steady 20-30% weekly
rhythm, adjusted up for a heavily stocked tank or down for a lightly stocked, heavily planted one, suits most
community setups better than any fixed universal number.

Treating tap water properly before it touches the tank is non-negotiable — municipal water supplies commonly
carry chlorine or chloramine specifically because those are effective at killing bacteria, which is exactly
why they are equally effective at harming both fish and the tank's own beneficial bacterial colony. A
conditioner rated for chloramine specifically (not just chlorine) is important, since simple dechlorinators
do not neutralise chloramine's ammonia component.

The one genuine exception to "regular water changes are always the safe default" is the pH-ammonia trap
(corpus:ph-ammonia-trap) — in a tank with both low pH and elevated ammonia, a normal water change can
convert relatively harmless ionised ammonia into its toxic form. Checking for that specific combination
before changing water is a small habit that avoids a real, if uncommon, risk.
