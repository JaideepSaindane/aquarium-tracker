symptoms:
  - Fish struggling to maintain normal swimming position — floating to the surface, sinking to the bottom, swimming sideways or upside down, or struggling to swim at all
  - Visible bloating/distended belly in some cases
  - Otherwise normal appetite and alertness in mild cases; lethargy and other systemic signs in more serious underlying-illness cases
likely_causes:
  - Constipation, often from overfeeding or a diet lacking fibre (especially common in fancy goldfish and bettas) — the most common, mildest, and most fixable cause
  - Gulping air while eating floating food, common in fish with an upturned mouth or when feeding at the surface aggressively
  - Bacterial or parasitic infection affecting the swim bladder or gut, causing genuine bloat rather than simple constipation
  - Genetic/anatomical predisposition in certain fancy-bred varieties (particularly round-bodied fancy goldfish) where the swim bladder is compressed by selective breeding for body shape
  - Rarely, an underlying organ issue or tumour, especially in an older fish with a persistent, worsening problem
immediate_actions:
  - Rule out simple constipation first, since it is by far the most common cause and the easiest to address — fast the fish for 24-48 hours, then offer a fibre-rich food (blanched peeled peas are the classic, genuinely useful recommendation for many species) rather than the fish's normal diet.
  - Check feeding method — if the fish is gulping air while eating floating pellets, consider pre-soaking food so it sinks, or switching to a sinking food.
  - Observe whether the issue resolves within a few days of the fasting-then-fibre approach — most constipation-based swim bladder issues do.
  - If the fish shows other signs of illness (lethargy, loss of appetite, visible bloating that doesn't respond to fasting) rather than just a buoyancy problem, treat this as a potential infection rather than simple constipation and consider corpus:bacterial-ulcers-septicaemia or general antiparasitic approaches depending on the fuller symptom picture.
do_not_do:
  - Do not assume every buoyancy problem is fixed by "feeding a pea" — this genuinely works for simple constipation but does nothing for an infection-based or anatomical cause, and repeatedly trying it on a fish with a different underlying problem just delays appropriate treatment.
  - Do not overfeed peas or fibre food as an ongoing default diet — it is a temporary corrective measure, not a balanced long-term diet.
  - Do not ignore a chronic, worsening swim bladder problem in an older fish as "just old age" without considering it may reflect a genuine internal illness worth investigating.
when_to_escalate: "A buoyancy problem that does not improve after a proper fasting-and-fibre attempt, especially with other signs of illness present — this points away from simple constipation toward an infection or anatomical cause that needs a different approach; an experienced keeper's assessment of the fuller picture is worthwhile before cycling through multiple unrelated treatments."

Swim bladder disorder is an umbrella symptom — a fish struggling with normal buoyancy or swimming
position — with causes ranging from trivially fixable (simple constipation, by far the most common) to
genuinely serious (bacterial infection, or an anatomical predisposition in some selectively bred fancy
varieties). The popular "feed a pea" advice is genuinely correct for the common case, which is exactly why
it gets applied indiscriminately to fish whose actual problem is something else entirely.

The practical approach starts with the simple, low-risk intervention: a short fast followed by fibre-rich
food, which resolves constipation-based cases within a few days in most fish. If that does not resolve the
problem, or if the fish shows other signs of illness alongside the buoyancy issue — lethargy, loss of
appetite, worsening rather than improving — the underlying cause is more likely infection or something
structural, and needs to be investigated on its own terms rather than repeating the pea treatment
indefinitely.

Round-bodied fancy goldfish varieties deserve a specific mention: their swim bladders are often compressed
by the same selective breeding that produces their distinctive body shape, and some degree of buoyancy
difficulty in these fish may be a lifelong anatomical trait rather than a treatable illness at all.
