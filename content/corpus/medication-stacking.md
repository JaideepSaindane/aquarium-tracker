Stacking multiple medications — dosing two or more treatments into the same tank at once, whether to cover
several possible diagnoses simultaneously or out of impatience with a first treatment that has not shown
results yet — is, in practice, the single most common way a keeper accidentally kills an entire tank rather
than saving it.

Two mechanisms make this so dangerous. First, medication interactions are frequently untested and
undocumented for any specific combination — a product being individually safe at its labelled dose says
nothing about what happens when it is combined with a second, different chemical in the same water.
Second, and often the more immediately lethal factor in practice, is cumulative oxygen demand: several
medications together, especially anything affecting gill tissue or metabolic rate, commonly exceed what any
individual product's label safety testing accounts for, and fish frequently die of the combined oxygen
stress rather than from a specific toxic interaction.

The correct approach is disciplined and slower than stacking feels like it should be: identify one likely
problem, treat it with one appropriate medication for its full intended course, and only move to a
different treatment once that course is genuinely finished or clearly not the right diagnosis — not
because a few days have passed with no visible change yet.
